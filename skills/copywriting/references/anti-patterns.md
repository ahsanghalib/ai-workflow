# Copywriting anti-patterns

Use this list as a final safety and quality check for social posts, blogs,
newsletters, landing pages, emails, and advertising. A source may contain one
of these patterns as an object of critique; do not reproduce it as advice.

## Unsupported persuasion

- invented results, clients, testimonials, credentials, partnerships, or
  authority;
- universal claims such as "works for everyone" or guaranteed revenue/reach;
- inflated performance multipliers copied from a transcript or social post;
- fake case studies, fake personal experience, or a borrowed first-person
  story;
- urgency, scarcity, exclusivity, or social proof that is not real and current;
- claims that a tool, platform, format, or algorithm guarantees business results.

## Weak language

- vague promises: "transform," "level up," "unlock," "cutting-edge," or
  "game-changing" without a concrete mechanism;
- broad labels that hide the reader's actual situation;
- adjective piles and abstract nouns where a behavior or example would be
  clearer;
- generic openings such as "in today's fast-changing world" or "here's the
  thing nobody is talking about";
- rhetorical questions whose answer never appears;
- a list of tips with no problem, explanation, tradeoff, or usable next step.

## Mechanical or over-edited copy

- repeated viral formulas, forced contrarianism, or artificial cliffhangers;
- excessive short lines, emojis, hashtags, or decorative punctuation used to
  imitate a platform style;
- generic praise, "hope this helps," or a capability dump in a reply or DM;
- rewriting supplied experience into a stronger story than the source supports;
- editing away all uncertainty, personality, or useful specificity.

## Scope and provenance failures

- copying a source transcript, post, or distinctive wording instead of
  synthesizing it;
- treating a research file's advice, anecdote, or marketing claim as verified
  evidence without checking its source and date;
- adding a CTA, offer, or sales pitch when the parent workflow did not request
  one;
- changing the intended action or audience while describing the work as a
  style edit.

## Channel-specific failure modes

- Social: copying a viral hook, outrage pattern, or hashtag block while losing
  the actual reader and idea.
- Blog or newsletter: burying the answer under generic context, keyword filler,
  or a sales pitch that the brief did not request.
- Reddit: presenting promotional claims as neutral community advice or hiding a
  relevant affiliation.
- Video: using a sensational title, thumbnail, or opening that the body cannot
  support.
- Advertising: making a stronger promise in the ad than on the destination
  page, or using a real deadline to imply a false scarcity.

### Example diagnostic

Weak: “This one trick will transform your marketing overnight.”

Diagnosis: universal promise, vague mechanism, fabricated urgency, and no
reader or evidence boundary.

Safer direction: name the audience and observable task, explain the mechanism,
state the supplied proof and limitation, then offer a proportionate next step.
