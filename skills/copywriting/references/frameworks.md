# Copywriting framework library

Read this reference when the user asks for a copywriting formula, when the
artifact needs a deliberate structure, or when the first draft feels
unfocused. Frameworks are scaffolds for reasoning. They are not guarantees of
attention, persuasion, conversion, ranking, or revenue.

## Selection rule

1. Freeze the reader, problem, evidence, offer, and action first.
2. Choose the smallest pattern that matches the reader's awareness and the
   artifact's job.
3. Use one primary framework. Add a second only as a diagnostic, such as FAB
   inside an offer section or the “so what?” test during editing.
4. Replace every promise, benefit, proof point, and urgency device with
   supplied or checkable material.
5. Remove the framework if it makes the copy predictable, manipulative, or
   less clear than a direct explanation.

## Core patterns

### AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action

Use when a reader needs a gradual path from first contact to a next step.

- **Attention:** a specific problem, observation, contrast, or opportunity;
- **Interest:** relevant context, mechanism, example, or explanation;
- **Desire:** a bounded outcome, fit, or reason to care;
- **Action:** the smallest proportionate next step.

For an educational post or article, “desire” may be a desire to understand or
try an idea, not a product pitch. Attention must earn the next line; it must not
be bait.

### PAS: problem, consequence, solution

Use when the reader already feels a recognizable problem. “Agitation” should
mean a real consequence, tradeoff, cost, or unresolved tension—not fear,
shame, invented urgency, or exaggerated pain.

- **Problem:** name the situation in the reader's language;
- **Consequence:** show what the problem affects when the evidence supports it;
- **Solution:** explain the mechanism, fit, limitation, and next step.

If the problem is not established, use an observation or question instead of
pretending to know the reader's pain.

### BAB: before, after, bridge

Use for a case study, transformation explanation, landing page, or short social
post where the contrast clarifies the decision.

- **Before:** the current situation, constraint, or workaround;
- **After:** a plausible improved state under stated conditions;
- **Bridge:** the actual action, mechanism, or offer connecting the two.

The “after” is a scenario or supplied result, not a guarantee. The bridge must
explain what changes rather than simply naming a product.

### FAB: feature, advantage, benefit

Use for product, service, proposal, or advertisement copy when the reader needs
to understand why a capability matters.

- **Feature:** what exists or is included;
- **Advantage:** what the feature changes in the process or comparison;
- **Benefit:** why that change matters to this reader in this situation.

Label an inferred benefit as an inference. Do not convert a feature into a
measured outcome without proof.

### PPPP: picture, promise, proof, push

Use for a page or campaign that needs a future state followed by evidence and
an action. A promise must be bounded; the picture must be plausible; proof must
precede the push; and the push must match trust and readiness.

Do not add testimonials, statistics, guarantees, bonuses, limited availability,
or deadlines unless the parent workflow supplies them and a separate review
approves their use.

## Diagnostic frameworks

### The Four Cs

Use **clear, concise, compelling, credible** as a final editorial gate rather
than a rigid sequence. “Compelling” means relevant and worth the reader's
attention. “Credible” means bounded, inspectable, and honest about uncertainty.

### The “so what?” test

Ask of every important sentence: “Why should this reader care?” Keep the
sentence, make its implication explicit, move it nearer to the relevant fact,
or remove it. Do not answer “so what?” with an invented benefit.

### The question ladder

Use for a blog, newsletter, guide, or educational video. Start with the
reader's primary question, then order the likely follow-up questions by what is
needed to understand or decide next. Use supplied questions, support data,
approved research, or clearly labeled hypotheses. Do not add keyword padding or
pretend that search volume proves reader intent.

### Funnel-stage alignment

Use awareness as a planning hypothesis:

- **Discovering:** explain the problem or opportunity and build understanding;
- **Considering:** compare mechanisms, alternatives, proof, and tradeoffs;
- **Deciding:** reduce risk with fit, evidence, limitations, and a clear action.

Do not force every asset into a funnel stage or infer a reader's stage from a
single click, view, or demographic label.

## Patterns to use conditionally or reject as defaults

- **CARI-style competitor and research workflows:** useful for an SEO or
  content-research parent workflow, but competitor inspection and current
  search research are not copywriting evidence by themselves.
- **Refresh, intent, deepen, update, and restructure workflows:** useful when
  maintaining an existing blog or page, provided fact freshness, search intent,
  links, and technical SEO are checked by the appropriate workflow.
- **PAS plus benefits plus optimization:** use the PAS and FAB pieces when they
  fit; do not add “optimization” as a decorative final step.
- **SLAP or other attention-first acronyms:** use only if each step can be
  stated plainly and improves the actual reader decision. Capitalization,
  emojis, shock, and compression are not a strategy.
- **Scarcity, warning, and “act now” sequences:** use only for a real,
  disclosed deadline, dependency, or availability constraint. Never create
  pressure to compensate for weak proof.
- **Contrast patterns:** old/new, before/after, problem/solution, and
  common/personal can generate angles. The contrast must be true, relevant, and
  specific; do not invent an enemy or manufacture a contrarian position.
